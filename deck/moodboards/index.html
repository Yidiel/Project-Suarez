Streetwear

Salt Lake is for the weird

Salt Lake has a secret everybody in the scene already knows: this is a punk city. The straight edge hardcore scene here was nationally famous in the 90s, SLC Punk! put a blue mohawk on the state in 1998, and Kilby Court has been running all ages shows out of a garage since 1999. That is the Salt Lake we sell, not the ski poster. The merch logic is band merch logic. Heavyweight Beefy-T blanks, one color back prints, tour date lists, sleeve hits on longsleeves, bootleg type that looks xeroxed three generations deep. Graphics come from flyers taped to a door, not a brand guide. Treatments are oil wash, bleach, pigment dye, distressed cotton, safety pins and studs as hardware. Photography is flash on a porch, a polaroid at a show, a kid in a bodega. Pleasures, Stray Rats, Online Ceramics and Brain Dead prove you can do this at retail without losing the spit. For Visit Salt Lake the move is a venue-tour tee for a city, not a logo on a cap.

Performance

We run on Salt Lake

Salt Lake is not a ski town with a city attached. It is a training ground with a zip code. People here get up at 5 to run the Bonneville Shoreline before work, skin up Alta in the dark, climb granite in Little Cottonwood on a lunch break, and ride the canyon road until their legs quit. They race the Wasatch 100 and the Speedgoat because the mountain is right there and so is the clock. This line should feel like race kit, not resort merch. Think bibs pinned to a singlet, laser perforation where you sweat, bonded hems, a reflective hit that only shows up in headlights, split times printed like a hard copy line from a 1990 Nike ad. Honest fabrics shown close up: Alpha Direct grid, mesh, ripstop. Photography is cold light, breath, dirt, effort. Nothing cute. The payoff line is simple: Salt Lake is performance. We just make the gear that proves it.

Objects

The unseen objects of a Salt Lake day

The unseen objects of a Salt Lake day, designed like they matter. Not souvenirs, not mugs. The stuff you actually touch between the airport and the lift line: the ice scraper in the trunk, the goggle case in your glovebox, the Zippo and matchbook at the bar, the rack your skis hang on all week, the record you put on when the snow is coming down. Hermès petit h and Loewe prove a scrap of leather or a lump of clay can carry a whole house. Aimé Leon Dore, Kith and Supreme prove a lighter or an ashtray can be the hero SKU. A24 proves a candle label or a vinyl sleeve can be a brand voice. We borrow that care and point it at Salt Lake's daily rituals, with the lake, the brine shrimp, the salt crust and the Alta chairlift as the raw material. Hard light, one object, no props. If a local would not use it on a Tuesday in February, it is not in the line.

Neo-Western

Rancher at 7,000 feet

Here is the pitch. Western is having its biggest moment in decades, but the version winning right now is not costume cowboy. It is Bode cutting rodeo tailoring from old quilts, Pharrell putting fringe and turquoise on a Louis Vuitton runway, and Native-owned Ginew lining a chore coat with a Pendleton blanket an Osage artist designed. The trick is restraint: one chain stitched motif on a back yoke, pearl snaps on a cuff, a chenille flower, a bolo on a plain shirt. Salt Lake earns this lane honestly. We have the Days of '47 Rodeo and a longhorn drive through downtown, the Antelope Island bison roundup, sheep and cattle in Heber and Sanpete, cowboy poetry, paintbrush in Albion Basin and aspen gold in the fall. So the merch reads like a working Utah ranch shirt that a design kid would wear in the city. Serif and rodeo script type, Wasatch sunset palette, natural light photography, no cartoon cowboys. Workwear first, embroidery second, nostalgia last.

Latino

Latin life in the West

This lane is not "Salt Lake does Hispanic Heritage Month." It is Latin life as it is already lived in the West: a lowrider parked under the Wasatch, a quinceañera posing in the Capitol rotunda, Chapulín cosplay at a Mexico match at Rice-Eccles, a jaripeo at the Fairpark during Days of '47. The words on the shirts come from the community, not from a tourism office, so no invented taglines. We borrow the merch logic that already works: Born x Raised gothic script and jersey block type, Kids of Immigrants plainspoken slogans, Paisa Boys fútbol crests, Bad Bunny's residency that turned a coquí into a tee. Paired with rótulo sign painting and lotería color, that is the type system. Photography should follow Estevan Oriol: honest, high contrast, in the neighborhood. The line has to be designed by Latino designers from Salt Lake County, co-signed by West Valley, Rose Park and Glendale, with a revenue share going to a community org like Centro Civico Mexicano or Comunidades Unidas. If the community would not wear it to a Sunday car show, we cut it.
