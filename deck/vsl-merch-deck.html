Base and Meridian

A merch line for Visit Salt Lake. Working name. Round 1, October 28, 2026.

1

Working name: Base and Meridian. Every Salt Lake address is a coordinate measured from the base-and-meridian marker set by the 1855 survey at the southeast corner of Temple Square (verify), so '400 South 200 East' is already copy, it sounds like a label rather than a campaign, and it gives the empty Sub-Brands section of the brand guide its first entry while 'Salt Lake' stays the word on the garment. It is civic survey heritage, not a church symbol, but it sits next to Temple Square, so the team should be comfortable with that, and it needs a trademark search. Alternates: Terminal Lake (the Great Salt Lake has no outlet, and the airport terminal is ten minutes from downtown; dry and funny, but it can read grim next to the 2022 record low), Brine (the water, the shrimp harvest, the industry; short and streetwear-ready, but a crowded word), and 0,0 (the origin point as numerals; great on a cap, hard to say out loud or search).

What merch is now.

The brands people collect stopped treating merch as a logo on a blank. Here's who does it well, what failed, and what we take from them.

5

Part 1 runs through luxury, streetwear, tech, outdoor, destination, sports, the Utah scene and hospitality, then closes with ten principles. The thread through all of it is Highsnobiety's line that 'anything can be turned into merch if infused with enough symbolic value and cultural capital.' A city starts with more of that than any software company does.

Merch became the luxury product.

  • Hermès petit h makes one-of-one objects from leftover leather, silk and porcelain, from about $70 to $70,000.
  • Hermès Off-Piste skis, October 2025: sycamore over an ash core, $19,300. The house sells the sport its customers actually do.
  • Loewe turned a 2024 Instagram joke into a real tomato clutch in nappa lambskin.
  • Miu Miu grew retail 35% in FY2025 while spending on culture, like Summer Reads books in city-specific colors with ex-libris stamps.

AnOther and Nuvo on petit h · Teton Gravity Research, 2025 · Tatler Asia on Loewe, 2024 · The Industry on Miu Miu, 2026

6

At the top of the market nobody calls it merch. Hermès gives the $770 Rodeo charm and the $19,300 ski the same materials and story discipline, and petit h runs on 'creation in reverse': start from the offcut, then ask what it can become. Loewe shows craft is what makes a joke land, and Miu Miu shows a city-coded book can do the job of a logo. For us that means one standard of make from the cap to the jacket, or we don't make the cap. Hermès put skis in the catalog because its customers ski; a mountain city has more right to that category than Paris does.

Technique replaces the logo.

Bottega Veneta's woven intrecciato reads as Bottega with no wordmark. Stone Island claims over 60,000 garment-dye recipes. Satisfy puts holes in a cotton tee and calls it MothTech.

Fzine and 10 Magazine on Bottega, 2025 · Stone Island, About · Urban Industry on Satisfy · Gear Patrol on Carhartt

7

One line from the Bottega coverage sums it up: 'There doesn't need to be a logo to guess where the orange intrecciato gloves come from.' Louise Trotter's SS26 cut the strips to 3 mm, so the signature keeps moving without changing what it is. Stone Island's compass badge earned its place by sitting on fabrics nobody else made, and Carhartt WIP sells the Detroit Jacket shape at $248 to $268 against about $110 for the US original by changing the wash and the fit. The move for Salt Lake is to pick one construction detail (a chain stitch, a bar-tack color, a label shape) and make it recognizable, so the wordmark can stay small.

Outdoor got quieter and more specific.

The loud gorpcore years are over. What's left is one good shell, one good fleece, trail runners, and not talking about it.

Jing Daily, Feb 2026 · Outside, Sept 2026 · Jing Daily on the Arc'teryx Museum, 2024 · Glossy on Tracksmith

11

Yid named Nike ACG first, and the 2026 ACG is place-based again: an athlete department, named mountains, trail racing, and safety orange as the one accent. Outside's September 2026 headline, 'Gorpcore Is Dead. Long Live Outdoor Style,' marks the move to stone, navy, black and earth tones. Satisfy prices a MothTech hoodie at £239.95 and reviewers say it 'makes no excuses,' while Tracksmith's Matt Taylor says skipping wholesale markup lets them use fabric other brands can't. That rewards fabric and restraint over graphics, which is exactly where a city line can compete, and Salt Lake's buyer is the amateur who skis 20 days a year on a lunch break.

The best regional lines name real places and real makers.

  • Adidas Spezial names shoes after towns, Blackburn SPZL and Manchester 89, and launched in Manchester in 2014, not London. Gary Aspden: 'culture can exist anywhere.'
  • Snow Peak Local Wear limits each project to one regional producer, like Niigata and Iwate in 2024, and sells it in Japan only.
  • 66°North started in 1926 making gear for Icelandic fishermen. Its 2025 Fisherman's Print comes from the wear left on old coats by 'salt, storms and time at sea.'

Dazed and 032c on Spezial · WWD Japan on Snow Peak, 2024 · Hero and T3 on 66°North, 2025

12

Spezial is the model for naming: a town becomes a product name, and Aspden launched at Design Manchester on purpose, calling it contrarian. Snow Peak's rule, one regional producer per project and sold in that region, is the single most transferable idea in the outdoor research. 66°North shows a working origin (fishermen's gear, search and rescue from 1928) can carry a brand to a Regent Street flagship a century later. The Salt Lake version is piece names from places (Little Cottonwood, Antelope Island, Bonneville) and one drop a year made with a Utah maker and labeled that way.

Pittsburgh is the closest model for VSL: a public body, a trusted local maker, archival graphics, and a tag or decal that sets official pieces apart from the sea of unofficial ones. Only NY is the most credible city brand in America because it treats a parks leaf and a subway system like heritage marks; Salt Lake's equivalents are TRAX, the county parks and Antelope Island. Parks Project's Vans capsule gave $250,000 to the National Parks Conservation Association, which is why its vintage graphics feel earned.

Three ways city merch goes wrong.

Each one got friendlier, cheaper or more popular than the idea could hold.

  • We ❤️ NYC, March 2023. A refresh with a blocky sans and an emoji-style heart got mocked so hard that officials had to say it wasn't replacing Milton Glaser's original.
  • The $22 Aspen crewneck. The town name sells for $22 to $100 on Etsy, PacSun and Abercrombie. Once the mall has it, it stops proving you were there.
  • I amsterdam. The letters drew such crowds that the city pulled them from Museumplein on December 3, 2018.

Dezeen and Surface, 2023 · Etsy, PacSun, Abercrombie listings, 2026 · Dezeen, 2018

14

We ❤️ NYC looked like a committee's idea of friendly, while Glaser's original works because a real designer kept it restrained. Aspen is the airport test in action: when the name is the only content, anyone can print it, and it ends up as a bachelorette sweatshirt. I amsterdam is a different problem, a mark that succeeded so hard it became an overtourism symbol; the Salt Lake version of that tension is selling a shrinking lake without being seen to help it. So keep the type serious, put something on the garment besides the name, and tie a give-back to the lake.

Hotels, restaurants and museums sell proof you were there.

The SF Standard called August 2026 a 'restaurant merch renaissance,' past 'neoprene koozies and cheap ceramic mugs.' Erewhon's December 2024 winter line came in five named colorways (Tomato, Butterscotch, Plum, Coconut, Chaga) and a $150 tote went viral, while MoMA's curators vet every item in the Design Store. What people are picking up right now.

  • Real lettering over fake vintage. PRINT's 2026 Type Report says designers are leaving 'vintage' fonts for actual signage, packaging and regional lettering.
  • The premium trucker. Foam fronts are out. Cotton twill, corduroy and suede fronts are in.
  • Fewer, slower drops. Three to five pieces every six to eight weeks. A drop every two weeks 'turns scarcity into noise.'
  • Stores as clubhouses. Pop-ups, run clubs and third spaces. Six in ten Millennials want retail that mixes community and shopping.
  • Western, done quiet. Pearl snaps and a felt hat are back. Fringe and big buckles are out.

PRINT Type Report, 2026 · Foremost Hat on truckers · E-commerce Fast Lane on drops · Retail Technology Show, 2026 · Yahoo Shopping and E! on Western, 2026

18

These come from 2025 and 2026 trade and trend coverage, and each one points at a lane: real local lettering (State Street motel signs, trail markers, Fairpark signage) for streetwear and Latino, the trucker for Western, run clubs for performance. Another Cotton Lab's Sunday Running Club went from one Düsseldorf storefront to 2,000 runners at a night run. Photography is shifting too, toward catalog shots with film grain and light retouching, because heavy retouching now reads as dishonest to younger buyers. On Western, Utah has the real thing (REDWEST, the Fairpark, the Days of '47 Rodeo) more than anyone selling the trend.

What we take, part 1: the garment carries the idea.

  • Put the idea on it, not the logo. 'thinking' on a cap, 'I love you, say it back' on a hoodie. Ours should be something locals already say.
  • Name the author. Every drop credits a designer, ideally from Utah, the way A24 and Kilby Court do.
  • Print the edition size. Dior did 8,500, Fermat did 75, the Jazz did 300. The number goes on the hem tag.
  • Let the make be the logo. One technique and one named fabric per season, the way Bottega owns a weave and Satisfy owns MothTech.
  • Make one hero that isn't apparel. OpenAI's $70 basketball, the New Yorker tote, Pendleton's 1916 Glacier Park blanket.
19

These five are about the object itself, and they are the test every Part 3 piece has to pass: an idea, an author, a number, a recognizable make, and at least one non-garment hero per lane. Pendleton's park blankets have sold for over a century because the maker was real and the design was restrained. If a piece fails two of these, it is airport merch and gets cut.

What we take, part 2: run it like a label, not a gift shop.

  • License and curate. Like Pittsburgh, Only NY and Tokyo Tokyo, VSL owns the archive, the story and the official tag, and picks the maker.
  • Pull from the archive, not the logo file. Pittsburgh used an 1800s merchant flag. Salt Lake has Saltair postcards, the 1899 Salt Palace and the grid.
  • Locals first. Only NY and Detroit vs. Everybody sold to residents first. Visitors buy what locals wear.
  • Give it a room and a calendar. A shelf at a place like Fice, and a release every six to eight weeks tied to the city's real seasons.
  • Tie a real number to the place. A Great Salt Lake line without a measurable give to the lake is just pink.
20

These five are about how the program runs. Locals first is a deliberate break from the brand guide, which casts photo subjects 'as visitors, not locals'; the merch should dress the local and let visitors follow. The give-back has to be real and public, with partner terms published, because a public body gets read harder than Online Ceramics did over its Wikipedia deal. The parent brand is America's Mountain City™.

The platform says you don't have to choose between city and mountains. Every piece in the line maps to one of its three pillars.

  • Both at Once. City and mountain in the same place, no compromise. "A trail that ends two blocks from a sidewalk café."
  • Perpetual Momentum. Jazz, Mammoth, RSL, Royals, festivals and art. "Already different than the last time you were here."
  • Frictionless. "Forty minutes from gate to gondola." The airport is ten minutes from downtown.
  • What the merch inherits. The palette and its names, the salt crystal, the contour texture, Ivar and Saans, and a voice that is confident, playful, friendly and genuine.

Materials that signal: cotton weight, dye and fleece.

  • 6.5 oz and up is now the visible line between merch and clothes. A 5 oz promo blank reads as a free shirt.
  • Garment dye is the fastest way to a color story that isn't commodity, for $2 to $5 more per blank.
  • 14 oz cross-grain fleece for the hoodie. At 50 units a heavyweight hoodie lands at $34 to $45 (est.).
  • Made in USA blanks like Los Angeles Apparel roughly double the blank cost, but the claim is clean.

Research 05, blanks and makers, 2026

28

People feel the weight before they see the graphic, so the hero tee is a 6.5 oz or heavier garment-dyed blank: LAA 1801GD, Shaka 7.5 oz, Camber 8 oz, Lane Seven 16005. Comfort Colors is now the default nice souvenir tee, which makes it less of a signal. Custom Pantone dye lots need 300 to 500 units, so the 50-unit test uses stock garment-dyed colors and saves custom dye for a reorder. Utah has no large cut-and-sew blank mill, so 'Utah-made' really means Utah-designed and Utah-decorated.

Technical materials, and why not Gore-Tex.

  • Polartec Alpha Direct. Lofted insulation first built for US Special Operations. No liner, very breathable, snags easily. It's the Gain pullover.
  • Pertex Quantum Air. Light, tightly woven nylon that trades some wind resistance for breathability. It's the strip on Gain.
  • 3M Scotchlite reflective. A safety material doing a design job, $2 to $6 a placement (est.). It's Five A.M.
  • Gore-Tex isn't for sale. W. L. Gore licenses about 200 brands, and a DMO won't get a license.

Research 03, materials glossary, 2026 · Bergzeit Magazin on Gore-Tex licensing, via research 05

29

Fabric names now work as brand signals people read on a hangtag, so naming the mill is part of the design. Gore-Tex is a licensed-brand program with lab-certified styles, so the realistic options are Polartec, Pertex, Schoeller or an unbranded membrane, or a partner brand that already holds a license. Polartec has 300 to 500 yard per-color minimums, so at 50 units Gain goes through a converter that sells Alpha Direct by the yard. The PFAS phase-out also matters for a lake story: 'made without intentionally added PFAS' is table stakes on anything with a shell.

The two hard noes are the Olympic marks and the state's snow slogan, both already Yid's rules; the research explains why. 'Made in Utah' is a trap because no large Utah mill makes blanks, so almost everything is Utah-designed and Utah-decorated, and the FTC treats an origin claim on a website the same as one on a label; the Artemia pouch's draft inside stamp says 'Made in Utah' and has to change unless the maker can meet the standard. UBIT is why we recommend the licensing and royalty model: Visit Salt Lake is a nonprofit DMO (a 501(c)(6), verify), city souvenirs are the textbook UBIT example, and royalty income generally sits outside it.
