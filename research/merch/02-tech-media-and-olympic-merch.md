# How tech, media and Olympic brands turned merch into fashion

Research slice for the Visit Salt Lake merchandise strategy deck. Thesis under test: destination merch should carry the brand's ethos on the garment, not just a logo on a tee.

Research notes on method: findings below come from roughly 40 web searches run on October 26, 2026. Direct page fetches (CNN, Quartz, Fortune, the Hyper newsletter, Highsnobiety) were blocked by the sandbox network, so quotes are taken from search result snippets and are marked when they are paraphrased. Items marked "(from memory, verify)" are background knowledge not confirmed in this session. The search budget ran out before a final pass on 2002 licensing totals and merch KPI frameworks; those are flagged in the Olympic section.

## 1. Summary

The pattern across every category is the same. The brands whose merch gets worn in public, resold on Grailed, and written up by design press all treat merch as a product line with a point of view, not as a promotional expense. OpenAI's Supply Co. ($15 socks to a $175 half-zip, a $70 basketball) ties drops to company milestones and keeps an "archive" of decommissioned pieces like a fashion house. Perplexity Supply sells a $135 chore jacket under the line "quality goods made for curious minds." Anthropic sells nothing at all and still produced the most talked-about AI hat of 2025 by giving the "thinking" cap away with coffee at a NYC newsstand and letting eBay do the pricing. A24 built a merch business by hiring real designers (Online Ceramics, Nahmias, Brain Dead) and letting them make the thing they would make anyway, with the film as subject rather than the studio as logo. Venezia FC left Nike for Kappa specifically to get creative control, then sold 95% of its kits outside Italy. Paris 2024 won design praise by making a conceptual mascot (a hat, not an animal), hiring a real design director, and spreading licensing across 75 mostly small French makers. Salt Lake's own history proves the point: the single most remembered object from 2002 was a $19.95 Roots fleece beret that people lined up around the block in Park City to buy. For a 2034 host city, the lesson is to start the merch program as a brand program now, with named designers, local makers, limited runs, and products that say something about Utah rather than just spelling it.

## 2. Brand by brand

### Tech and AI companies

**Anthropic / Claude.** There is no public Anthropic or Claude store as of October 2026. Everything official is internal, event-only, or a giveaway. What exists:
- The "thinking" cap: a washed navy dad cap with lowercase "thinking" stitched on the front. It ties to the "Keep thinking" brand campaign Anthropic launched with the agency Mother in September 2025. In October 2025 Claude handed out free thinking caps and coffee at a NYC newsstand; one Substack writer titled her post "2 Hours in Line for a Free Hat." The caps were never sold; eBay listings show one seller moving 117 units at $49.99 and unworn ones listed at $80. A Singapore merch blog called it "the AI merch campaign nobody planned." Sources: https://edatweets.substack.com/p/2-hours-in-line-for-a-free-hat, https://gumtoo.sg/blog/anthropic-thinking-cap-ai-merch-viral-campaign, https://perkupapp.com/post/anthropic-swag-a-closer-look
- Code with Claude conference: attendees got branded mechanical keyboard keycaps marked Anthropic and Claude.
- Project Vend phase two: Anthropic ran an internal experiment where an AI agent called "Clothius" designed custom T-shirts, hats, socks and swag to employee specs. Most popular item overall: an Anthropic-branded stress ball. Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/project-vend-2
- An August 2026 Seoul Economic Daily headline referenced an "Anthropic pen for $39.99" as a merch premium driven by IPO anticipation, but I could not confirm the item or who sells it. Source: https://en.sedaily.com/international/2026/08/05/anthropic-pen-for-3999-the-merch-premium-built-by-ai-ipo
- Design agency: nothing public names an agency behind Claude merch. The brand campaign agency is Mother. Treat the merch as in-house.
- Why designers like it: scarcity plus restraint. One word, no logo, one colorway. The product is the idea ("thinking") not the company name. This is the clearest example in the whole set of "ethos on the garment."

**OpenAI Supply Co.** Started as an employee-only store, opened to the public in late July 2026 with 10 items. Prices: $15 ribbed socks, $40 T-shirt, $40 Blossom Hat, $40 Codex Bubble Cap, $70 ChatGPT basketball, $175 half-zip. A $230 Codex Creator Micro keyboard with Work Louder shows up in one lower-reliability source. Ben King, a design director at OpenAI, told press the basketball came from the Golden State Warriors arena facing their SF office, that Virgil Abloh's work was a touchstone, and that releases will track company milestones instead of fashion seasons. The store has an "archive" section listing past items with "decommissioned" dates. Several items sold out in days; a Codex cap and hoodie set resold for $475. Ben King's stated goal (CNN, Aug 2026, paraphrased from snippet): cool, thoughtful items that reflect the product while engaging the company's own community. Reception was split: Inc. ran "Who Asked for This?" and Shopifreaks noted "fashion-world mockery," while resale prices say demand is real. Sources: https://qz.com/openai-supply-co-merch-store-public-launch-072326, https://openai.com/supply/shop/, https://www.inc.com/rebecca-deczynski/who-asked-for-this-openai-is-the-latest-tech-giant-to-get-in-the-merch-game/91376125, https://www.shopifreaks.com/openai-opens-its-supply-co-merch-store-to-the-public-with-15-socks-and-a-175-half-zip-drawing-fashion-world-mockery/
- Lessons: the archive is a brilliant move (it makes every drop collectible by definition), milestone-based drops beat seasonal calendars for a non-fashion brand, and a hero object that is not apparel (the basketball) got more press than any tee.

**Perplexity Supply.** Standalone storefront at perplexity.supply with the line "quality goods made for curious minds, designed by Perplexity." Products: Curious Tee $29, Curious Hoodie $62, Know It All Hoodie $62, Curious Chore Jacket $135 in "Ecru" or "Wonder Blue," Comet Hoodie with a reflective 3M logo (sold out at times). The whole line is built on phrases about curiosity and learning rather than the logo. A designer who worked on it told CNN (anonymously) the work was like the billboards on Highway 101, "ubiquitous and sometimes indiscernible," which is a fair self-critique: the pieces look like Silicon Valley, not like Perplexity. Sources: https://perplexity.supply/, https://edition.cnn.com/2026/08/17/style/tech-ai-merch
- Lesson: naming colorways ("Wonder Blue") and building copy around a value (curiosity) is cheap and makes a line feel authored. The risk is generic "tech core" where every company's hoodie looks the same.

**The CNN frame (Aug 17, 2026, "Long after Steve Jobs' turtleneck, there's a new tech uniform").** From Palantir to OpenAI, companies are "producing fashion merch in an attempt to soften their image with cotton and canvas." Critics in the piece: merch can camouflage the controversial parts of AI companies, and much of it "lacks a clear point of view." That critique is the exact gap a destination brand can exploit, because a place has a point of view built in. Source: https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/17/style/tech-ai-merch

**Vercel.** Launched vercel.store as its own public merch shop in mid October 2026, the same week it moved its commerce product vercel.shop to general availability (Shopify's Brennan Loh posted "corp merch game hitting new highs"). Before that, v0 hats and hoodies were visible in the community but not for sale, which generated a Vercel Community thread asking when they would be. Source: https://x.com/bloh/status/2111234013872406666, https://community.vercel.com/t/plans-on-releasing-v0-merch/10639
- Lesson: the merch store is a product demo. Vercel is selling the shop as much as the hoodie.

**Figma / Config.** Figma's store (store.figma.com) sells limited edition tees with 100% of profits going to carbon removal through TIST, a tree-planting program. Config is the real merch engine: Figma's Brand Studio starts the visual identity about 10 months out and it "encompasses everything from main-stage screens and spatial experiences, to attendee badges and sought-after swag." For Config 2025 they commissioned Maryland artist Ian Mackay to design an off-brand "bootleg" tee, which is the kind of self-aware move designers share. TikTok is full of Config merch haul videos. Sources: https://www.figma.com/blog/how-we-shaped-the-visual-identity-for-config-2025/, https://store.figma.com
- Lesson: event merch is the easiest place to take creative risks (an audience that is already a fan, a one-time run), and commissioning an outside artist to do a "bootleg" is a repeatable format.

**Stripe / Stripe Press.** Stripe Press (launched 2018) publishes and redesigns books under the line "ideas for progress," which Axios tied to Stripe's mission to "grow the GDP of the internet." The job listing for Stripe's Editorial Design team says the team "designs books, campaigns, and merchandise to build awareness of the brand." The merch logic: the product is a beautifully made object with ideas in it, and the Stripe name is a small colophon. Nobody calls a Stripe Press book "swag." Sources: https://www.axios.com/2018/07/17/payments-company-stripe-is-getting-into-book-publishing, https://jobs.generalcatalyst.com/companies/stripe/jobs/38364114-editorial-designer-stripe-press
- Lesson for a destination: a Salt Lake "press" (a field guide, a trail atlas, a ski history) can be merch. Objects that carry content outlast objects that carry logos.

**Ramp.** merch.ramp.com, tagline "modern merch made for Ramp." The line is built entirely on finance jokes: a "Future CFO" onesie, a baby "Security Blanket," a puzzle and umbrella with finance-pun names. Source: https://merch.ramp.com/
- Lesson: humor that only your audience fully gets is a form of belonging.

**Linear, Cursor, Midjourney, Notion, Substack, Arc, Cash App, Coinbase.** Searches turned up no credible reporting on official programs for these in this session; most results were third-party Redbubble and Etsy knockoffs, which itself is a signal (people want to wear these brands). Linear is widely admired by designers for a restrained merch set (heavyweight black hoodies and caps with tiny marks) but I could not verify current store details (from memory, verify). The Browser Company is winding Arc down (sunset Dec 2026) and being acquired by Atlassian, so skip. Cash App's past collabs with streetwear designers exist in memory but were not confirmed here (from memory, verify).

**Fermat / FRMT and the Hyper case study.** Hyper (hyperstudios.us, the brand strategy newsletter Oren John runs with a partner) wrote "How we turned a SaaS startup into a designer brand" about Fermat Commerce, a B2B commerce software company. The direct page would not load in this environment, so this is reconstructed from snippets and related posts:
- Framing from the post: the story of helping Fermat make "the hardest capsule collection in B2B marketing," which started with a call from Rabah Rahil, Fermat's CMO, who "wanted to make some merch."
- The line was named FRMT and called a "limited edition Couture Line": five ecommerce-inspired pieces, described in Hyper's own words as a "luxury capsule collection called FRMT." It was sold through Fermat's own funnels (the product Fermat sells is shoppable landing pages, so the merch demoed the software). Scarcity was explicit: "we only have 75 of each piece available for purchase, and we already have pre-orders placed." Pieces were also seeded through giveaways.
- Rahil, on a marketing podcast, framed it as "why most brand merch fails" and how Fermat "flipped the script by creating products people actually wanted."
- Hyper's companion post, "How to make better merch" (by Sprezza), is the practical framework. From snippets and from how Hyper writes: treat merch as a fashion product (fit, fabric weight, construction) before treating it as marketing; design the thing you would buy at full price with the logo removed; constrain the run so it carries status; let the brand's actual subject (for Fermat, ecommerce) be the graphic content; and launch it like a fashion drop (teaser, unveil, launch, sustain), which is the four-beat structure Oren John teaches in his campaign masterclass.
- Sources: https://mail.hyperstudios.us/p/how-we-turned-a-saas-startup-into-a-designer-brand, https://www.hyperstudios.us/p/how-to-make-better-merch, https://podimo.com/en/shows/marketing-people-love/episode/55fc212f-82d3-50cb-af5c-db8bc308b24d, https://x.com/orenmeetsworld/status/2001023364538962335
- The transferable framework in one line: a boring company became a "designer brand" by making five real garments, capping them at 75 units, and selling them through the same channel the company is famous for.

### Sports clubs, teams and leagues

**Venezia FC.** The clearest "club as fashion label" case. Ted Philipakos (chief brand officer, later left for Athens Kallithea): "We left Nike for Kappa in part because we like Kappa as an Italian brand with a rich heritage and a strong production capability, but above all we made the switch to gain full creative control." And: "Where some clubs may depend on agencies, we direct everything ourselves, from the design process to the editorial campaigns." Creative direction by Fly Nowhere (Diego Moscosoni), later kits by Bureau Borsche. The home kit sold out on day one; 95% of online sales came from outside Italy. The website ran essays about the city and interviews with cultural figures. A Nocta (Drake and Nike) kit deal followed. Sources: https://versus.uk.com/articles/how-venezia-fc-became-the-worlds-most-fashion-forward-football-club, https://highsnobiety.com/p/venezia-fc-kappa-third-kit-gold-interview, https://www.soccerscene.com.au/venezia-fc-cultivating-a-unique-fashion-and-branding-profile/, https://www.nssmag.com/en/sports/34503/venezia-jersey-kappa-borsche

## 3. Principles for a destination brand

1. Put the idea on the garment, not the logo. Anthropic's "thinking" cap, Perplexity's "Curious," Ramp's "Future CFO." For Salt Lake, the word or phrase matters more than the wordmark.
2. Hire an author. A24 credits Online Ceramics, Nahmias, Brain Dead on the product page. Every Visit Salt Lake drop should have a named designer or studio, ideally a Utah one (Actual Source in Provo is the obvious first call, verify availability).
3. Cap the run and say so. Fermat did 75 units per piece. The Jazz sold 300 and ran a drawing. Scarcity is what turns a tee into a thing people line up for.
4. Build an archive. OpenAI lists decommissioned items with dates. That makes every drop a collectible and gives the store a history page. Start one on day one, and backfill it with 2002.
5. Drop on milestones, not seasons. OpenAI ties releases to company events. For a DMO: Sundance week, the first snow, Pride, Outdoor Retailer, the 2034 countdown marks (Utah 2034 launched merch at the 3,000 days mark).
6. Make one hero object that is not apparel. OpenAI's $70 basketball, Criterion's toy truck, the New Yorker tote, the Roots beret. For Salt Lake: a beanie or a pin set is the obvious hero.
7. Price like a label, not like a promo house. Tees $40 to $60, hoodies $100 to $120, outerwear $175 to $250 are the ranges every praised program sits in. Cheap merch signals cheap brand.
8. Keep one giveaway channel. Anthropic sold nothing and still won. A free object given at the right place (a newsstand, the airport, a trailhead) with a line out the door is a media event.
9. Let the resale market be your KPI. Grailed, eBay and StockX prices for A24, Jazz City Edition, OpenAI Codex sets and the Roots beret are the honest measure of brand love. Track them.
10. Demo the destination through the product. Vercel's store runs on Vercel. Fermat sold FRMT through Fermat pages. Visit Salt Lake merch should be sold where visitors already are (airport, hotels, Utah Olympic Park) and tied to a visit, not just a web cart.
11. Sell the city, not the org. Venezia FC's site ran essays about Venice; 95% of sales came from abroad. The brand on the label should be Salt Lake, with Visit Salt Lake as the small woven tag inside.
12. Use local makers as licensees. Paris 2024 spread licensing across 75 mostly small French companies and highlighted a Brittany plush maker when "made in China" criticism hit. NPR uses RAYGUN. NYC Parks uses Only NY.
13. Give collaborators a platform. A24 lets costume designers and makeup artists extend their work into product. Salt Lake equivalents: ski photographers, Sundance poster artists, Navajo and Ute makers, local breweries and coffee roasters.
14. Hire merch leadership from fashion or footwear. Netflix hired a Nike VP. A promo-products vendor will not get you to A24.
15. Expect the mockery and ship anyway. OpenAI's launch drew "who asked for this" headlines and sold out. The Utah 2034 logo was mocked (Gov. Cox: it "united people, though not in a good way") and the merch still launched.

## 4. Olympic merch lessons for a 2034 host city

**Salt Lake 2002: the Roots beret.** The Team USA beret by Canadian outfitter Roots, worn by the US delegation at the opening ceremony, retailed for $19.95. ABC4's 20-year retrospective: it was "one of the hottest souvenirs of 2002," and "the line of folks looking to grab one at the Roots boutique in Park City" went "out the door and down the street." It was briefly a scalper item and now sells on eBay for about $10, with tagged ones at $15 to $30 on Poshmark and Etsy. Widely reported at the time: Roots sold over a million berets (from memory, verify, the search budget ran out before confirming the total). Pin trading was the other 2002 merch culture, and pins remain the most collected 2002 object. Sources: https://www.abc4.com/news/digital-exclusives/20-years-later-remembering-some-of-the-key-venues-keepsakes-from-the-2002-olympics-in-salt-lake/, https://www.ebay.com/itm/376791266632
- Lesson: the most remembered object from the last Salt Lake Games was a soft, cheap, slightly odd hat worn by athletes on TV, sold in a single boutique with a line outside. It was a garment with a story (the athletes wore it) and a price anyone could pay. That is the brief for 2034.

**Paris 2024.** What worked:
- A real design director (Joachim Roncin, previously creative director of the Ballon d'Or and the César awards) and a conceptual mascot: the Phryge, a Phrygian cap, chosen to "stand out from the trend of animal-inspired mascots" and to express freedom. The look of the Games used abstract forms and typography that avoided French clichés.
- 75 licensees, mostly small French firms, returning a share of revenue to the organizing committee; 140 official boutiques; a committee target of around 2 billion merchandise products and roughly 130 million euros net from licensing (targets, not audited results; final figures were not published in anything I found). Sales of official products reportedly doubled each month in early 2024. The 38 cm Phryge plush was often out of stock and was Carrefour's best seller.
- Fashion capsules: Lacoste "Heritage Paris 1924" ($70 to $195), Maje, Sandro, Pandora (nine-piece jewelry capsule sold in official stores), Ben Sherman for Team GB, Chubbies for Team USA ($39.50 to $89.50), and the Jordan 4 "Wet Cement" Paris pack, which became the top-traded Olympic item on StockX (8,373 trades).
- Problems: 600 counterfeit Phryge plushes seized in March 2024, and criticism that most plush was made in China (the committee answered by pointing to a Brittany producer).

**What this means for Visit Salt Lake specifically.**
- The official Games merch is locked to Fanatics, Mitchell & Ness and the USOPC system, and it will look like every other Games. Visit Salt Lake cannot use the rings, but it can own the city layer that the official program will not touch: the "host city" aesthetic, the 2002 nostalgia, the ski-town uniform.
- Start a "2002 archive" drop now: a licensed or re-imagined beret, pin sets, the 2002 color palette, with a local designer. The nostalgia market already exists on eBay and Poshmark.
- Position for 2029: when the final emblem lands, Visit Salt Lake should already have a named designer roster, local licensees (Utah print shops, knitters, pin makers), a flagship retail location (airport plus a downtown pop-up like Milano's Duomo store), and an archive page.
- Copy Paris on makers and LA on social enterprise: a Utah version of Homeboy (refugee or Native owned makers) as first licensee is a press story and a values story.
- Copy Roots on the hero object: one soft, affordable, slightly weird winter hat, worn by someone on TV, sold in one line-out-the-door location.
