# 05. Production logistics, manufacturing, economics, and the audience

Research slice for the Visit Salt Lake merch strategy deck. Thesis of the deck: destination merch should carry the city's ethos on the garment. This file covers how it actually gets made, what it costs, how long it takes, how a nonprofit DMO can sell it, and who buys it.

Sourcing note: web search for this pass was capped partway through and direct page fetches failed on this network, so some figures below come from search snippets rather than the original pages, and a number of rows are flagged **(est.)** meaning industry rule-of-thumb from trade experience rather than a cited 2025 price sheet. Every estimate should be replaced with a written quote before anything goes in a budget. Numbers with a source are marked with the source in the row or a bracketed cite that maps to section 8.

## 1. Summary

The production side of fashion-forward destination merch splits into three lanes, and the deck should be explicit about which lane each product sits in. Lane one is **decorated premium blanks**: a domestic screen printer or embroiderer decorating a heavyweight blank from Los Angeles Apparel, Shaka Wear, Comfort Colors, AS Colour, Stanley/Stella, Lane Seven, House of Blanks, Independent or Camber. This is where 80 percent of the "cool merch" people admire actually lives, it runs at 24 to 72 piece minimums per design, lands at roughly $9 to $30 per finished unit depending on blank and technique, and turns around in 3 to 6 weeks. Lane two is **cut-and-sew / private label**: a Los Angeles studio like Bomme, Lefty or Indie Source, or a Portuguese or Japanese factory, building the garment from a tech pack with custom labels, hardware and fit. MOQs of 50 to 300 per style, $25 to $90+ landed per unit for fleece and tees, 4 to 9 months from concept to first drop. Lane three is **technical outerwear** (shells, fleece, insulated), which almost always means a sourcing agent and an Asian or Portuguese technical factory, MOQs of 300 to 500 per style and color, $60 to $150+ per unit, and 9 to 14 months. Gore-Tex is a licensed-brand program, not a fabric you can buy, so a DMO would use Polartec, Pertex, Schoeller or an unbranded membrane, or partner with a licensed Utah brand.

Economics: wholesale-to-retail pricing in this category runs 2x to 2.6x landed cost (keystone and above), and direct-to-consumer brands like Aimé Leon Dore, Satisfy and A24 price heavyweight tees at $45 to $75, hoodies at $120 to $200 and caps at $40 to $65, which is 3x to 5x landed cost because they skip the wholesale layer. A DMO selling direct through a visitor center, airport and Shopify can run those DTC multiples on a modest 200 to 500 piece run and still clear 55 to 70 percent gross margin. Print-on-demand (Printful, Printify) is a zero-inventory fallback for long-tail designs, but it caps you at commodity blanks and 20 to 40 percent margins and should not be the hero product.

Legal: "The Greatest Snow on Earth" is a federally registered mark owned by the State of Utah (Reg. 3031199, 2005, renewed), so use needs a license from the state. Olympic marks are off limits without USOPC/IOC permission under the Ted Stevens Act, including "Olympic", the rings, "SLC 2034" combinations and parodies like "Saltlympics". Merch sales by a nonprofit DMO are likely taxable (Utah sales tax) and may generate unrelated business income depending on how the mission connection is framed, so finance and counsel should be in the room early.

Audience: Salt Lake has a large and unusually young, outdoor-oriented visitor base to sell into. SLC International handled 28.16 million passengers in 2025, Utah resorts logged 6.5 million skier visits in 2024-25, Sundance drew 85,472 in-person attendees in 2025 (33 percent out of state), and Utah visitor spending hit a record $13.7 billion in 2025 with Visit Salt Lake citing $6.15 billion of annual visitor spending in Salt Lake County. On the demand side, PPAI data says 63 percent of Gen Z have bought something because of merch and 62.6 percent rank apparel as their favorite merch category; Skyscanner's 2026 trends work finds 23 percent of travelers made a trip in the past year specifically to buy something unavailable online, with Gen Z most proud of limited-edition destination totes; and ACI reports Gen Z and millennials spend 3.5x more in airport retail than Gen X and boomers. The insider signal ("you had to be there") is the mechanism that converts a visitor into a repeat wearer and a local into a buyer.

Takeaways for the deck:

- The hero tee should be a 6.5 oz or heavier garment-dyed blank (LAA 1801GD, Camber 301, Shaka 7.5 oz, Lane Seven 16005). That weight is now the visible line between "merch" and "clothes".
- Made in USA is available at every tier (LAA, Camber, Bayside, Royal) but roughly doubles blank cost versus the import equivalent. Utah has no large cut-and-sew blank mill, so "Utah-made" will mean Utah-designed and Utah-decorated unless you go cut-and-sew locally (see section 6).
- Garment dye (Comfort Colors, LAA GD, Stanley/Stella's dyed line) is the fastest way to get a non-commodity color story, and allows custom Pantone dye lots at 300 to 500 units (est.) with LAA or a Los Angeles dye house.

Fabric and brand licensing realities:

- **Gore-Tex** is not purchasable fabric. W. L. Gore licenses roughly 200 brands worldwide and evaluates whether a new licensee would grow the overall Gore-Tex pie before granting one; the licensee's product developers go through Gore training and every style is lab-certified [S9]. A DMO will not get a Gore license. Options: partner with a Utah brand that already has one (none of the large Utah outdoor companies hold a current Gore apparel license that I could confirm; Black Diamond has used Gore in the past, verify), or use an alternative.
- **Polartec** sells to brands through its mill but with per-color yardage minimums and typically prefers to work through existing cut-and-sew customers; small brands piggyback on a factory that already stocks Polartec, which is the normal route (est.). Polartec is made in Lawrence, Massachusetts and Cleveland, Tennessee, which supports a "US fabric" story even if sewing is offshore.
- **Pertex, Schoeller, eVent, Sympatex, Toray Dermizax, Polartec NeoShell** are the usual Gore substitutes at the brand level; unbranded 2.5L and 3L PU membranes from Taiwan and Japan are the small-run route.
- **bluesign** and **OEKO-TEX** are fabric-level certifications you inherit from the mill; ask for the certificate numbers in the first conversation.

### Pricing and margin

- **Wholesale multiplier**: brand wholesale price is typically 2x to 2.5x landed cost. **Retail multiplier**: retailers mark wholesale up 2x to 2.2x ("keystone" is exactly 2x; 2.2x to 2.5x is common in fashion boutiques). So a $12 landed tee becomes $26 to $30 wholesale and $55 to $65 at a boutique. **DTC**: when the brand sells direct it keeps both layers, so the same $12 tee at $48 retail is a 75 percent gross margin.
- **Reference retail pricing (2025 to 2026, from brand sites, est. where noted)**: Aimé Leon Dore heavyweight logo tee $75 to $95, hoodie $190 to $240, cap $55 to $75. Satisfy running tee $110 to $160 (technical fabrics), cap $70 to $95, MothTech tee $130. A24 tees $40 to $60, hoodies $90 to $130, caps $40 to $50 (A24 prices like a record label, not a fashion house, and pairs "big film, small designer" so each film gets its own merch cycle [S19]). Business of Fashion notes that music and media merch is now designed with coveted labels and sold at higher price points than before [S19]. Tourism gift shops: typical airport or visitor-center tee $24.99 to $34.99, hoodie $54.99 to $69.99, cap $24.99 to $29.99 (est. from the category), usually on Gildan/Comfort Colors blanks at 3x to 4x cost.
- **Suggested DMO price ladder** (est.): tee $42 to $55, long sleeve $55 to $65, hoodie $95 to $125, crewneck $85 to $110, cap $38 to $48, beanie $32 to $42, fleece $140 to $180, shell $220 to $300, chain-stitch one-offs $150 to $350. At the landed costs in the table this is a 55 to 72 percent gross margin direct, and still 25 to 40 percent if wholesaled to local shops at 50 percent off retail.
- **Break-even sketch** (est.): a 250-piece first drop across tee, hoodie, cap and beanie (say 100 tees, 50 hoodies, 60 caps, 40 beanies) costs roughly $4,500 to $6,500 landed including labels and tags. At the price ladder above it retails for $14,000 to $17,500. Add $1,500 to $3,000 for photography and $500 to $1,500 for packaging and you are still at a 2.5x to 3x return on cash at sell-through, with sell-through being the only real risk.
- **Print-on-demand economics**: Printful margins 20 to 30 percent, Printify 30 to 40 percent, with Printify about $5 cheaper per basic shirt but quality that varies by which print provider fills the order; Printful is the steadier option because it runs its own facilities; both use essentially the same DTG equipment [S20]. POD is fine for a long tail of neighborhood or event designs; it is not fine for the hero line because the blanks are Bella 3001 and Gildan, the print is DTG, and a $35 POD tee leaves $7.

## 5. Timeline and the first manufacturer conversation

### Realistic calendar

**Lane one, decorated premium blanks (domestic decorator):**

| Step | Duration |
|---|---|
| Concept, art, blank selection, order sample blanks in 3 colors | 2 to 3 weeks |
| Decorator quote, art separations, digitizing, pre-production strike-off or embroidery sew-out | 1 to 2 weeks |
| Approve, order blanks (distributors ship in 1 to 3 days; LAA and Camber direct 1 to 3 weeks) | 1 to 3 weeks |
| Production | 1 to 3 weeks |
| Labels, tags, bagging, ship to you | 1 week |
| **Total concept to product in hand** | **6 to 10 weeks** |

**Lane two, domestic cut-and-sew:**

| Step | Duration |
|---|---|
| Design, tech pack, fabric and trim sourcing | 4 to 6 weeks |
| Pattern and first proto sample | 3 to 5 weeks (proto samples typically 7 to 14 days once the factory starts, plus queue) [S21] |
| Fit review, revisions, second sample | 2 to 4 weeks (fit samples add 5 to 10 days, pre-production samples another 5 to 7) [S21] |
| Pre-production sample approval, bulk fabric arrives | 2 to 6 weeks (fabric is the long pole) |
| Bulk production | 4 to 8 weeks (30 to 60 days after sample approval is typical for US brands) [S21] |
| QC, finishing, labels, ship | 1 to 2 weeks |
| **Total** | **4 to 7 months** |

**Lane three, overseas cut-and-sew or technical:**

| Step | Duration |
|---|---|
| Design, tech pack, agent onboarding, fabric sourcing | 6 to 8 weeks |
| Proto, fit, PP sample rounds, each with 1 to 2 weeks courier time | 8 to 14 weeks |
| Fabric lead time from mills (overseas mills quote 8 to 24 weeks) [S21] | overlaps, but drives the critical path |
| Bulk production | 6 to 10 weeks |
| QC inspection (third party, $300 to $600 per day) | 1 week |
| Sea freight to Salt Lake via LA/Long Beach or Seattle, plus customs | 5 to 8 weeks (air: 1 to 2 weeks at 3x to 5x the cost) |
| **Total** | **7 to 12 months; 9 to 14 for technical outerwear** |

A good tech pack cuts average sample rounds from about 4.5 to 1.8 per one seller's data [S21]; that is the single biggest schedule lever.

Back-plan for Salt Lake: a holiday / ski-season drop wants product in hand by early November, so Lane one starts by late August, Lane two by April, Lane three by the previous December. A Sundance (late January) capsule on Lane one starts by mid-November.

### What a tech pack contains

1. Cover sheet: style name and number, season, brand, date, version, designer contact.
2. Technical flat sketches, front and back, with callouts for every seam, stitch type, closure, pocket, label position and decoration placement (not mood-board art) [S21].
3. Bill of materials: fabric (content, weight in GSM, mill, color/Pantone TPX or TCX, finish), every trim (thread, labels, zippers with brand and size, drawcords, aglets, snaps, eyelets, elastic), packaging.
4. Construction details: stitch types (coverstitch, flatlock, overlock), seams per inch, seam allowance, bar tacks, topstitch widths, seam taping spec for shells.
5. Graded measurement spec (points of measure for each size, with tolerances), and the base size for the sample.
6. Colorways and artwork files: vector art (AI/EPS/PDF), placement and dimensions, Pantone callouts for ink, thread color numbers (Madeira or Isacord), embroidery DST file if you have it.
7. Labeling and care: main label, size label, care and content label (US law requires fiber content, country of origin and care instructions, RN number or company name), hangtag spec.
8. Packaging and folding instructions, polybag spec, carton marks.
9. Fit comments log and sample history (added as rounds happen).

### First manufacturer conversation checklist

Bring these or expect the quote to be meaningless:

- Spec sheet or tech pack (even a draft) and reference garments you like ("fits like a House of Blanks 500 GSM hoodie").
- Quantities per style, per color, per size, for the first order and the expected reorder cadence.
- Target landed cost and target retail price per style.
- Timeline with the drop date and the date you need product in Salt Lake.
- Artwork files in vector, Pantone references, thread color numbers, decoration placement with dimensions.
- Size range and spec (or the blank you want them to match).
- Fabric preference (content, weight, dyed or garment-dyed, certifications wanted: GOTS, OEKO-TEX, bluesign).
- Labels and packaging: who supplies woven labels and hangtags (you or them), bagging spec.
- Country of origin and claims you intend to make ("Made in USA", "Made in Portugal", "Designed in Salt Lake City").
- Questions for them: MOQ per style and color, sample cost and whether it is credited against bulk, payment terms (typically 50 percent deposit, 50 percent before ship; domestic decorators often net 30 for nonprofits with a PO), lead time from PP approval, QC process, who owns the pattern, what happens to overage and seconds, tariff exposure and HTS code if overseas.

## 6. DMO operations and legal

### Inventory vs on demand

- **Inventory (recommended for the hero line)**: buy 200 to 500 pieces per drop, hold at the visitor center and a 3PL or your own stockroom. Risk is unsold stock; mitigation is small first runs and a fast reorder path on decorated blanks (6 to 10 weeks).
- **Print-on-demand (long tail only)**: Printful (own facilities, steadier quality, 20 to 30 percent margin) or Printify (provider network, cheaper by about $5 per basic shirt, 30 to 40 percent margin, variable quality, split shipments when items come from different providers) [S20]. Use for neighborhood, trail, and event one-offs where you would never hold stock. Shopify integration is native to both.
- **Hybrid**: Printful also does bulk and warehousing, so one vendor can hold inventory for the hero line and print the long tail.

### Fulfillment

- **Shopify**: Basic plan is roughly $39/month (annual pricing lower), 2.9 percent + 30 cents per online card transaction, with Shopify POS for in-person sales at the visitor center. (Plan prices as of 2025, verify.)
- **3PL**: small-brand 3PLs (ShipBob, ShipMonk, regional Utah 3PLs along the I-15 corridor) charge roughly $2.50 to $4.00 pick and pack per order, $0.50 to $1.00 per additional item, $15 to $40 per pallet or $0.75 to $2.00 per bin per month storage, plus postage (est.). At under 300 orders a month it is usually cheaper to fulfill from the visitor center with a part-time staffer and Shopify Shipping labels.
- **Returns**: merch returns run 5 to 10 percent online, under 2 percent in person (est.). Sizing charts and a generous exchange policy matter more for a heavyweight boxy fit that people are not used to.

### Where to sell, physically

- **Visitor center** (downtown). Lowest friction, you control the display, staff can tell the story.
- **SLC International Airport**: concessions are run by airport concessionaires (Hudson, Paradies Lagardère, local partners like Hip & Humble, Salt Lake Running, and local-brand kiosks in the new terminal). A DMO can approach the concessionaire for a consignment or wholesale placement, or the airport's "local brand" program. 28.16 million passengers in 2025 [S22], and ACI's work says around 70 percent of airport purchases are impulse-driven and Gen Z and millennials spend 3.5x more than Gen X and boomers in airport retail [S23]. Airport per-passenger retail spend globally is soft (Kearney: travelers spending less per head, revenue up only because traffic is up) which means concessionaires are hungry for products that are not perfume and candy [S23].
- **Salt Palace Convention Center**: a pop-up at registration for major conventions. Visit Salt Lake has historically reported roughly 650,000 direct convention and event attendees in a year (2019: 655,289 attendees, $330.6 million direct spending) [S24]. Convention attendees buy "proof of trip" and expense-able gifts.
- **Hotels**: lobby retail at the Grand America, Kimpton Monaco, Asher Adams, Hyatt Regency; typically consignment at 60/40 or wholesale.
- **Ski resorts and Park City**: resort retail is run by Vail, Alterra and the independents; a co-branded piece is the entry point.
- **Local shops**: wholesale to Salt Lake boutiques and gear shops (the kind of stores that already carry Ebbets or Fairends) at 50 percent of retail, net 30. Consignment at 60/40 (shop keeps 40) is the lower-risk way to test.
- **Pop-ups at events**: Sundance (85,472 in-person attendees in 2025, 33 percent out of state) [S25], Outdoor Retailer when it is in town, Twilight Concert Series, Pride, the Utah Jazz and Real Salt Lake partnerships.

### Collab and licensing models

1. **DMO owns and sells** (highest margin, highest ops burden).
2. **License a local brand to make and sell it** (Visit Salt Lake supplies the marks and story, a Utah apparel brand designs, produces, and pays a 8 to 15 percent royalty on wholesale, est.). Lowest burden, lowest margin, biggest reach into the brand's existing channels.
3. **Revenue-share collab** (co-branded capsule with a local brand or a national label with Utah ties; 50/50 on net after cost is common for small capsules, est.). Best for credibility and press.
4. **Designer commission** (pay a local designer or artist a flat fee of $1,500 to $5,000 plus 5 to 10 percent royalty, DMO produces) (est.). This is the A24 playbook.

### Legal and tax flags (general information, not legal advice)

- **"The Greatest Snow on Earth"**: federal registration 3031199, registered December 20, 2005, owned by the State of Utah, status registered and renewed; first use claimed 1966; registered as a Utah state trademark in 1975; survived Ringling Bros. litigation (4th Circuit, 1999); the state filed "THE GREATEST SNOW ON EARTH UTAH" in 2018 (serial 88181452) [S26]. The state licenses it to Ski Utah and the Utah Office of Tourism and it appears on license plates. Any use on merch needs a license from the state (the Utah Office of Tourism is the practical contact). The mark is for tourism-promotion services, so the state would likely need a Class 25 (apparel) registration or license structure to cover merch; counsel should check.
- **Utah state brand marks ("Life Elevated", "Forever Mighty", the Utah logotype)**: the Utah Office of Tourism manages these under its brand style guides [S27]. No public merch licensing policy was found; assume written permission is required and that the office will want brand-guide compliance.
- **Olympic marks**: the Ted Stevens Olympic and Amateur Sports Act gives the USOPC exclusive rights to "Olympic", "Olympiad", "Paralympic", the rings, the torch, "Team USA", and Games marks, and does not require proof of consumer confusion [S28]. The USOPC does not allow unaffiliated third parties to use the marks commercially; it bans simulations of the rings (its examples include snowflakes and triangles arranged as rings) and wordplay like "Chicagolympics" [S28]. "2034" alone is a number and not protected, but "Salt Lake City 2034" with Olympic context, the SLC-UT 2034 organizing committee's logo and wordmarks, and anything implying sponsorship are off limits. The organizing committee will run its own licensing program closer to the Games, usually with licensees paying royalties of 10 to 15 percent (est.). What a host city DMO can do: celebrate winter sport, ski heritage, 2002 nostalgia in generic terms, and pursue an official "destination partner" or licensing arrangement with the organizing committee and USOPC. What it cannot do: put "Olympic", the rings, or "2034 Winter Games" on a shirt without a deal.
- **Sales tax**: Utah sales tax applies to retail sales of tangible goods; a nonprofit's purchases may be exempt but its retail sales generally are not. The DMO needs a Utah sales tax license for in-person and online sales, and marketplace facilitator rules cover sales through Shopify only if Shopify is the seller of record (it is not; the DMO is). Verify with the Utah State Tax Commission.
- **Unrelated business income (federal)**: UBIT applies to net income from a trade or business that is regularly carried on and not substantially related to the exempt purpose; gift shop inventory is judged item by item under the fragmentation rule, where souvenirs of the city are the classic example of items that may generate UBIT [S29]. Visit Salt Lake's structure (a 501(c)(6) nonprofit DMO funded largely by Salt Lake County transient room tax, verify) means the "destination promotion" mission argument is strong for apparel that literally promotes the destination, but the IRS tests primary purpose: if the primary purpose is income generation, UBIT exposure rises [S29]. A licensing/royalty model (passive royalty income is generally excluded from UBIT) sidesteps much of this, which is a real argument for model 2 above. Get a CPA opinion.
- **Labeling**: US apparel must carry fiber content, country of origin, care instructions and the manufacturer identity (RN number) under the Textile and Care Labeling rules.
- **Made in USA**: the FTC's Made in USA Labeling Rule (2021, 16 CFR Part 323) codifies the "all or virtually all" standard; penalties up to roughly $53,000 per violation; the FTC has pursued cases based on website banners, not just labels (Chaucer/Bates Accessories, 2023); in 2024 it announced two actions and 10 closing letters; a 2026 executive order directs the FTC to prioritize enforcement [S30]. "Made in USA" on an LAA or Camber blank decorated in Utah is defensible. "Made in USA" on a Shaka or Lane Seven blank is not. Use "Printed in Salt Lake City" or "Designed in Utah" where the blank is imported.
- **The "Utah-made" angle**: there is no large Utah cut-and-sew blank mill, but Utah has strong decoration (Salt Lake screen printers and embroiderers), outdoor-brand design talent (Black Diamond, Cotopaxi, Kuhl, Petzl America, Goal Zero, Backcountry.com, Pearl Izumi all have Utah operations), and small sewing shops. "Designed and decorated in Salt Lake City on Los Angeles Apparel cotton" is a truthful and strong claim. Cotopaxi's Del Día program (made from remnant fabric in the Philippines) is the local precedent for a sustainability story that is honest about geography.

### Sustainability shorthand

- **GOTS**: organic fiber plus processing chain; Stanley/Stella is the easiest GOTS blank.
- **OEKO-TEX Standard 100**: tested for harmful substances; most reputable blanks carry it.
- **bluesign**: chemical and resource management at the mill; Polartec and most technical fabrics carry it.
- **Fair Trade Certified / Fair Wear Foundation**: labor; Patagonia and Stanley/Stella respectively.
- **Deadstock fabric**: buy roll ends from LA jobbers (Fabscrap, Queen of Raw, LA fabric district) for capsules of 30 to 100 pieces; fits the small-batch, one-time story but can't reorder.
- Gen Z expects claims to be backed: 89 percent say eco claims on merch must be substantiated [S31].

## 7. Audience and demand

### Who buys fashion-forward merch and why

- **Merch as identity marker**: PPAI consumer research (cited by Quad, Bamko and Brandelity) finds 68 percent of Gen Z say merch positively affects brand perception and 63 percent say merch has influenced a purchase decision; apparel is the preferred merch category at 62.6 percent, followed by fashion accessories at 57 percent [S31]. Pacsun's Youth Report 2025 frames Gen Z purchases as tied directly to identity [S31]. GWI finds values and beliefs are the top driver of personal identity for Gen Z (59 percent) and age the 13th (30 percent), which supports selling a place's values rather than a place's name [S31].
- **The new souvenir**: Skyscanner's travel trends work (reported October 2026 as "pilgra-merch") found 23 percent of travelers made a trip in the past year to buy something they could not buy online; 28 percent of Gen Z are most proud of a limited-edition grocery tote from a destination; 22 percent of millennials show off branded robes or caps from hotels; a fashion psychologist calls it the latest iteration of "pics or it didn't happen" [S32]. Travel Blue launched destination-exclusive "Memory Collections" aimed at Gen Z, noting Gen Z buys impulsively and mostly in-store [S32]. The generic "I heart NYC" tee is described as losing appeal [S32].
- **The insider signal (IYKYK)**: the mechanism in all of the above is scarcity plus place. The tote is only available at the store, the cap is only available at the hotel. For a DMO the equivalent is a piece that is only sold in Salt Lake, references something only a visitor or local would get (a canyon, a trail name, a 2002 venue, the inversion, the lake's smell, the Wasatch Front), and is numbered or seasonal. Locals buy it as civic pride; visitors buy it as proof of trip; both wear it as a conversation starter in another city. That is the "earned merch" psychology: you had to be there.
- **Premium merch is now a fashion category**: Business of Fashion reports that artists, labels and festivals collaborate with coveted designers to make better-quality, fashion-forward merch at higher price points [S19]; A24's "big film, small designer" model and its cycle of a merch drop per film is the template for a DMO cycling drops per season or event [S19]. The Lyst Index shows how collab and cultural moments drive demand: Q2 2025's top product jumped 162 percent in searches on one celebrity moment; Adidas Sprinter shorts surged 121 percent; Stone Island re-entered the top 20 after four years [S33]. Lyst measures search, views, sales and social, which is the same engine a well-made city piece can tap when the right person is photographed in it.
- **Resale as a signal**: StockX, Grailed and Depop prices on scarce merch (festival, museum, media, and city-specific pieces) are the public proof of desirability; no specific resale datasets were pulled in this pass, so treat as a qualitative claim. The practical use for a DMO is to watch Depop and Grailed for "Salt Lake" and "Utah" searches to see what vintage Utah pieces already trade (2002 Olympic gear, Snowbird and Alta vintage, Utah Jazz 90s) and design toward that demand.
- **Secondhand and localism**: the State of Fashion 2026 (McKinsey and BoF, "When the Rules Change") reports 59 percent of global consumers are likely to buy secondhand in 2026 and that mid-market brands are overtaking luxury as value creators [S34]; the local, small-batch, well-made product fits both findings.
- **Gift shop and airport benchmarks**: the best museum data is old; AAMD's 2014 survey found most visitors spend under $8 per visit including admission and shop [S23]; one aggregator claims $4.50 shop revenue per visitor at major US art museums in 2023 (unverified) [S23]. Airport per-passenger retail spend: Heathrow £9.36 (about $12.65) in 2025, up 1.7 percent; Paris Extime €31.70, down 1.2 percent; Europe duty-free spend per passenger up 14 percent to $9.89 while Asia-Pacific fell 22 percent to $7.56 [S23]. Translation: the average passenger spends about $10 at the airport, so a $45 tee is bought by a minority of people with a reason, which is exactly the fashion-forward buyer, not the mass souvenir buyer.

### Locals vs visitors

- **Locals**: buy city pride and want to not look like a tourist. They respond to insider references, neighborhood names, the ethos line, and to being first (a drop at the visitor center with a line is a local event). Salt Lake County population is about 1.2 million with a median age around 32 (est. from census), one of the youngest metros in the country, with a high share of outdoor participants.
- **Visitors**: buy proof of trip, gifts, and something to wear on the plane home. They respond to quality (a hoodie they will actually keep), to a clear place mark, and to the story on the hangtag. Convention attendees have per-diems and buy at registration and the airport.
- Both segments converge on the same product if it passes the "would a local wear this" test. The product that fails is the one only a visitor would wear.
