# 08. SLC punk, from the late 70s to now, and what the scene is today

Research for the streetwear lane rebuild (slide 33 comment from the creative director, R1). Written October 29, 2026, with a verify pass the same day.

**How this was researched.** About 25 extended web searches. Page fetching (WebFetch) is blocked in this session for slugmag.com, cityweekly.net, deseret.com and saltlakehardcoreshows.com, so every fact here comes from search-engine summaries of the source pages, not full reads, plus research files 04 and 06. Where two sources disagree, both versions are given. Anything still unconfirmed is marked "(verify)".

## Summary

Salt Lake punk started small. In 1978 Spitting Teeth played a show in a University of Utah annex building, and by 1979 there were punk DJs on KRCL and a room called the Roxy under Sam Weller's bookstore on Main Street. Early-80s hardcore (Massacre Guys and a handful of others) played the Indian Center and, from 1984, the Speedway Cafe under the 500 South viaduct. The heavily LDS culture around it gave it a "we're the only weirdos here" identity, and that's the story *SLC Punk!* told later about fall 1985. Insight (1988 to 1990) was Salt Lake's first straight-edge band. By the late 90s Salt Lake had a national reputation for violent straight edge. Police called it a gang, there were stabbings, and a mink feed co-op, a McDonald's and a leather store were firebombed. In November 1998 a 15-year-old, Bernardo Repreza, was killed in a brawl with about 30 straight-edge kids. Musically the era's best-known band was Clear (1995 to 2000). Kilby Court opened in a garage in 1999 and changed the shape of the thing. Once there was a permanent all-ages room, the scene drifted toward indie, emo and post-hardcore. The Used came out of Orem, Velour gave Provo its own all-ages room in 2006, and Gaza, Eagle Twin, SubRosa and Cult Leader made Salt Lake a name in heavy music. In 2008 Kilby's founder sold it to S&S Presents, which grew Kilby into Kilby Block Party, now a Fairpark festival expecting about 30,000 a day. Right now (2025 to 2026) there isn't one "SLC punk scene." There's a small, active, mostly all-ages DIY hardcore circuit, much of it at Fellowship Hall, a sober-community hall in Sugar House. There's a punk bar circuit at Aces High Saloon, a big indie and festival layer run by S&S, and SLUG Magazine still covering all of it. It's small. What makes it distinct isn't size. It's all-ages access, the straight-edge legacy, DIY habits, playing against the dominant culture, and the skate and snow crossover.

## The scene now (2024 to 2026)

**What it actually is.** It's a cluster of small scenes, not one big one.

- **Hardcore is the most active punk-lineage thing, and it's DIY and mostly all-ages.** The main room is **Fellowship Hall** on Windsor Street in Sugar House, a sober-community hall (listed as "Fellowship Hall Clean & Sober" and "Sober Hall") that rents to shows. That's a fitting home for a straight-edge-rooted scene. Listed 2026 bills include Spent (January 23), cherem, Absolved, Sewn Shut and xForever Warx (February 13), and SPY, Empty Shell Casing, Home Front and Mask (October 26, Wild West Booking). **Black Lung Society** had a February 25, 2026 bill (Firestarter, Time Heist, xForever Warx, Tingle). **Olifer** and **Pineyard** show up only in 2025 listings. An Olifer listing says "PCP (Last Show)," which is the band's last show, not necessarily the room's, so whether those two are still running is unconfirmed (verify). **Hardcore Halloween at Kilby** (cover sets: Spent as Down to Nothing, Service Weapon as Backtrack, Mummy as System of a Down) is confirmed for October 12, 2025, and the 2026 edition is unconfirmed (verify). The volunteer listings site saltlakehardcoreshows.com looks stale, with nothing newer than 2025 indexed.
- **Honest note.** In April 2026 police investigated an assault that put a woman in the hospital at a metal show at Fellowship Hall. The hall said it didn't organize the event, just rented the space (ABC4). The scene isn't violent the way it was in 1998, but it's not a brochure either.
- **Current local hardcore bands (confirmed by a source).** **Bounty Hunter** (formed 2024; EP *On a Warpath*, mid-2026; No Echo interview). **Hollow Like Her** (SLC hardcore; EP *Through the Darkness*, June 1, 2026, independent). **Taste of Blood** (No Echo new-band spotlight; EP *456*). **xForever Warx** (Utah metallic hardcore; No Echo interview on their EP). **Mask** (on 2025 and 2026 bills; whether they're local is unconfirmed, verify). TECOVA's *Comfortable* got a SLUG local review in February 2026 as a hardcore-leaning record.
- **Punk is smaller and lives in bars and DIY.** **Aces High Saloon** (1588 South State) is the active punk and metal bar. It hosted a locals night on August 21, 2026 (Real Villains, Filth Lords, Ribbons, PoGoStiX, reviewed by SLUG) and The Bearded Punk Fest on September 12, 2026, with touring punk and metal booked into November and December 2026. SLUG's 2026 coverage names **The Corleones** (long-running, "a favorite among Salt Lake punk bands"), **Vicious Souls** (Localized feature, October 2026), **Lab Animals** (dance punk duo) and a September show with Soap Charge, DIY Suicide, PoGoStiX and Power Beer. City Weekly's 2026 music issue profiles **P.S. Destroy This** (punk, hardcore, riot grrrl). **The Beehive Social Club** (666 South State) had shows through spring 2026 (War On Women in March), but listings show nothing after May 2026, so its status is unclear (verify).
- **Metal has its own lineage and still draws.** Iceburn, Gaza, Eagle Twin, SubRosa and Cult Leader are the city's heavy music exports. SubRosa announced a reunion for Fire in the Mountains in July 2026, and whether it happened is unconfirmed (verify).
- **Indie and the festival layer are the big public face.** S&S Presents runs Kilby Court, Urban Lounge and Metro Music Hall, plus Kilby Block Party. That's what most people mean by "the Salt Lake scene." Turnstile on the 2026 bill and Deftones on 2027 show how hardcore and heavy music crossed into the indie festival world.
- **Touring hardcore and punk come through mid-size rooms.** Soundwell (Militarie Gun, October 4, 2025) and The Depot (Circle Jerks, October 8, 2025).
- **Promoters and collectives.** Hypha, Yardwork and Joz Presents (City Weekly 2026), Wild West Booking (Fellowship Hall bills), S&S Presents.
- **Labels, shops and press.** No big local label turned up. The one active punk label is **City of Dis** (punk label and distro on Bandcamp, with splits dated February and August 2026). **Raunch Records** in Sugar House is the long-running punk and hardcore record shop (four decades per Utah Stories, 2024). **SLUG Magazine** (issue 454, October 2026) is still the paper of record. It runs local reviews, the Localized series, skate coverage (Summer of Death, research file 06), the *Death by Salt* compilations and Craft Lake City. City Weekly does a yearly music issue.

**How big it is.** It's small, and saying so is part of the story. No source gives crowd counts for DIY shows. A hall show is probably a few dozen to a couple hundred people (estimate, verify with a promoter). Kilby Court's capacity wasn't sourced (verify). The one big number is Kilby Block Party at about 25,000 to 30,000 a day, which is an indie festival, not a punk crowd. Attendance numbers vary by source (one says 34,000 in 2026, one says 16,000 for an earlier year), so treat 30,000 a day as the festival's own estimate. Local musicians told City Weekly there are "so many shows happening always," which fits a small scene that's busy.

**What makes it distinct.**
1. **All-ages access.** From the Indian Center in the 80s, to The Moroccan and then Kilby Court's garage since 1999, to a sober hall in Sugar House now, the scene has always had a room for people too young for bars. That's why it keeps renewing.
2. **The straight-edge legacy.** Salt Lake is one of the few cities where straight edge is part of local identity (X'd hands, sober shows, a sober hall as the main DIY room). It also has a violent late-90s chapter that people still remember.
3. **DIY by necessity.** With few venues and strict liquor laws, people made their own spaces: crash pads, a garage, rented halls, a volunteer listings site, a magazine that started as a punk zine.
4. **Playing against the dominant culture.** Being the punk kid in a heavily LDS city is what *SLC Punk!* is about. Kerrang's "States of Metal" piece frames Utah's religious backdrop as what drives its heavy music, and SubRosa's Rebecca Vernon wrote about LDS policy directly. The point is pride and friction, not mocking anyone's faith.
5. **Mountain-town crossover.** Skate and snow sit next to the shows (Salty Peaks, Milosport and Saga's Summer of Death skate event per file 06). The "canyon in the morning, show at night" line is my characterization (verify with locals).

## Merch implications

1. **Draw from the 1978 to 1990 flyer era and the all-ages DIY habit, not from any one band.** That means photocopied flyers, hand lettering, cut-and-paste type, Leia Bell-style screen-print posters as a reference for craft (not to copy), one-color prints, the X'd hand as an "all ages" sign, and places as subjects: the viaduct, a garage, an alley, a rented hall. That fits what slide 33 already kept and what the monster tee and the hoodie do.
2. **Tell it as an evolution, and land on now.** The lane works because the scene is still alive and still all-ages. Kilby at 25, a sober hall full of hardcore kids, and a punk fest at Aces High are the proof. The 80s and 90s are background, so the pieces don't read as nostalgia for something gone.
3. **Don't romanticize 90s straight edge.** A boy was killed in 1998, and there were stabbings and firebombings. The X can be the all-ages hand stamp. Don't use crews, crew lettering, "hardline," weapons, or anything that reads as a gang mark.
4. **No real band names, logos, flyers, film art or venue marks without permission.** That includes Massacre Guys, *SLC Punk!*, Kilby, Turnstile and SLUG. Draw new work in the language. For real credibility, pay and credit local flyer artists and DIY promoters the way Kilby credits Chloe Monson and Tom Petersen, or partner on a small run with Kilby, SLUG or Craft Lake City.
5. **Keep it small and local, and be careful with the religious backdrop.** Small runs, sold first at a show, at Craft Lake City or at a shop like Raunch, match how the scene works. The "nothing to do here" humor is fine. Mocking LDS beliefs or symbols isn't, and a tourism office shouldn't do it.
