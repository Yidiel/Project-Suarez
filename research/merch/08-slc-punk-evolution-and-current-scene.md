## Summary

Salt Lake punk started as a small, scrappy early-80s hardcore scene (Massacre Guys and a few others) that played basements, the Indian Center and, from 1984, the Speedway Cafe under the 500 South viaduct. The religious culture around it gave it a "we're the only weirdos here" identity, and that's the story *SLC Punk!* told later about fall 1985. In the 90s the city got national notoriety for militant straight edge. Police called it a gang, there were stabbings, and the Fur Breeders co-op and other targets got firebombed (some of those arsons were tied to straight-edgers). In November 1998 a 15-year-old, Bernardo Repreza, was killed in a brawl with about 30 straight-edge kids, and *The Face* ran a feature on "Straight Edge Gangs in Salt Lake City" that same year. Kilby Court opened in 1999 and changed the shape of the thing. Once there was a permanent all-ages room in a garage, the scene drifted toward indie, emo, screamo and post-hardcore through the 2000s (verify specifics), and that path ends at Kilby Block Party, now a 25,000-to-30,000-a-day festival. Right now (2025 to 2026) there isn't one "SLC punk scene." There's a small, healthy, mostly all-ages DIY hardcore circuit (Fellowship Hall, Olifer, Black Lung Society, Pineyard, Hardcore Halloween at Kilby), a punk and riot grrrl edge (P.S. Destroy This), a big indie and festival layer run by S&S Presents, and promoters like Hypha, Yardwork and Joz Presents. It's small. What makes it distinct isn't size. It's all-ages access, the straight-edge legacy, DIY habits, playing against the dominant culture, and kids who skate and snowboard the same weekend they go to a show.

## The scene now (2024 to 2026)

**What it actually is.** It's a cluster of small scenes, not one big one. Here's my read, from the sources and with the gaps marked:

- **Hardcore is the most active punk-lineage thing, and it's DIY.** A volunteer-style listings site, saltlakehardcoreshows.com, was still posting shows through October 2025, at Fellowship Hall, Olifer, Black Lung Society, Pineyard and Kilby Court. A sample bill is Haywire, Mask, Taste of Blood and Bounty Hunter at Fellowship Hall in July 2025. Hardcore Halloween at Kilby on October 12, 2025 is the kind of yearly local fest this scene runs on. Who runs the site and how big the shows are is unconfirmed (verify). Whether these spaces are still open in late 2026 is also unconfirmed (verify).
- **Punk is around but smaller, and it overlaps riot grrrl and queer DIY.** P.S. Destroy This is the one current band City Weekly named for 2026. Other active local punk bands weren't named in what I could reach (verify with SLUG's local reviews and City Weekly's music issue).
- **Metal and heavy music have their own circuit**, historically at Club DV8 and later bars like Aces High Saloon and Beehive Social Club (verify both are open and still booking heavy shows). This research didn't confirm current venues.
- **Indie and the festival layer are the big public face.** S&S Presents, with Kilby Court, Urban Lounge (verify current ownership) and Kilby Block Party, is what most people mean when they say "the Salt Lake scene." Kilby Block Party booking Turnstile in 2026 shows how hardcore crossed into the indie festival world nationally, and Salt Lake shows it clearly.
- **Touring hardcore and punk come through mid-size rooms.** Soundwell and The Depot hosted Militarie Gun and Circle Jerks in October 2025. Metro Music Hall also books local and touring shows (verify current bookings). Quarters is an arcade bar, not a known punk room (verify; don't name it in the deck).
- **Promoters and collectives:** Hypha, Yardwork and Joz Presents (City Weekly 2026), S&S Presents (research file 06). Local labels weren't confirmed by any source I could reach (verify).
- **Press:** SLUG Magazine is still the scene's paper of record, a free monthly with local reviews, skate coverage (Summer of Death, research file 06) and its Craft Lake City DIY festival (verify). City Weekly does a yearly music issue.

**How big it is.** It's small. Saying so is part of the story. A good DIY hardcore show in a hall is a few dozen to maybe a couple hundred people (estimate, verify). Kilby Court is a small room (capacity not sourced, verify). The one big number is Kilby Block Party at 25,000 to 30,000 a day, and that's an indie festival, not a punk crowd. Local musicians told City Weekly there are "so many shows happening always," which fits a small scene that's busy.

**What makes it distinct.**
1. **All-ages access.** From the Indian Center in the 80s to Kilby Court since 1999 to the DIY halls now, the scene has always been built for people too young for bars. That's why it keeps renewing itself.
2. **The straight-edge legacy.** Salt Lake is one of the few cities where straight edge is part of local identity (X'd hands, sober shows), and it carries a violent 90s chapter that people here still remember.
3. **DIY by necessity.** With few venues and strict liquor rules, people made their own spaces: the crash pads, the garage, the halls, the listings site.
4. **Playing against the dominant culture.** Being the punk kid in a heavily LDS city is what *SLC Punk!* is about, and it still shapes how locals talk about the scene. That's a source of pride, not a joke at anyone's expense.
5. **Mountain-town crossover.** Skate and snow sit next to the shows (Salty Peaks, Milosport and Saga's Summer of Death skate event per file 06). The same kid goes to the canyon in the morning and a hall show at night (characterization, verify with locals).

## Merch implications

1. **Draw from the 80s flyer era and the all-ages DIY habit, not from any one band.** The visual language is photocopied flyers, hand lettering, cut-and-paste type, a one-color print, the X'd hand as an "all ages" sign, and the Speedway's viaduct and Kilby's alley as places. That fits what slide 33 already kept (flyers, zines, xerox grain) and what the monster tee and hoodie already do.
2. **Tell it as evolution, and land on now.** The lane works because the scene is still alive and still all-ages. Lead with Kilby Court and the current DIY halls as the living proof, and use the 80s and 90s as background, so the pieces don't read as nostalgia for a scene that's gone.
3. **Don't romanticize 90s straight edge.** A boy was killed in 1998, and there were stabbings and firebombings. The X can show up as the all-ages hand stamp. Avoid crews, hardline, violence imagery, "SLC SxE" crew lettering, or anything that looks like a gang mark.
4. **No real band names, logos, flyers or film art without permission.** Don't use Massacre Guys, *SLC Punk!*, Kilby or Turnstile marks, and don't copy actual flyers. Draw new work in the language. If we want real scene credibility, pay and credit local flyer artists and DIY promoters the way Kilby credits Chloe Monson and Tom Petersen, or partner with a venue or SLUG on a capsule.
5. **Keep it small and local, and be careful with the religious backdrop.** Small runs, sold first at a show or a local shop, match how the scene works. The "nothing to do here" humor is fine. Mocking LDS beliefs or symbols isn't, and a tourism office shouldn't do it.
