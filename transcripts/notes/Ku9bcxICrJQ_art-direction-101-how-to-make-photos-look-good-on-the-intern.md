# Art Direction 101 (how to make photos look good on the internet)

URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ku9bcxICrJQ

Length: about 23:30

## The main idea

Anyone can art direct a photo if every shot has at least one element that breaks from the ordinary. Oren gives a four-part cheat sheet (composition, action, location, lighting) plus post-production as a bonus lever, then backs it with reference boards and practice through copy work. The same cheat sheet doubles as the structure of a brief you hand a photographer.

## Frameworks and steps

- The one thing rule (1:00): each photo needs a single concept that sets it apart from two people standing and smiling. A feed of normal images makes one odd detail stop the scroll.
- Composition (1:30): how the frame is built. Unusual angles, framing devices, wide lens, distance.
- Action and activities (1:40): for non-photographers this matters most. People doing something (eating, holding food, interacting, jumping, playing a game) carry built-in pull. Give talent something to do with their hands.
- Location (2:40): if you know it ahead of time, study prior shoots there and scout angles and textures.
- Lighting (2:55): the “pro” layer. Basics like half-lit faces, making sure light lands on the subject, and being deliberate about the look.
- Cheat sheet in practice (3:05): with friends, run through composition, activity, location, light. With a hired photographer, bring location photos, lighting refs, composition refs and activity ideas so they can plan lenses, rigs and flash vs natural light. That bundle is basically a moodboard.
- Post-production as an extra tool (16:30): effects, vintage ad layouts, scale play (giant hands, oversized product), compositing white-background shots into a graphic.
- Copy work (18:10): replicate an existing piece step by step with your own person, place and story. For video, log each shot and its duration, then reshoot to the same timing and angles.

## Examples and case studies he names

- Cosmos, Are.na, Pinterest: where he keeps reference boards by category; his Cosmos boards are public.
- His own Valuable brand shoots: a diner overhead reference used for an upcoming shoot; a studio shot made interesting by having the model read a book, with a self-aware tone.
- A brand shoot (he credits its art direction highly) with models playing a game whose pieces echo the brand logo, lit well and shot from above. His example of action, symbolism and light combined.
- Bodega, screen-print lab, gas station with a chair, furniture in a pickup bed, garment rack dragged outside: objects out of place create the cognitive dissonance that stops scrolling.
- The Row in LA: he keeps a reference set of its concrete, garden and levels to brief new photographers fast.
- Artlist (sponsor segment, 11:30): stock footage and royalty-free music for quick social ads, assembled in CapCut with product cutouts and a vintage-ad layout.
- Garrett King: photographer with a dozen-plus lighting breakdowns; recommended study source.
- Edward Crockett: another photo creator to follow.
- Wedding photographers in his Cut30 program: a niche where tutorial content spreads well because both photographers and wedding parties seek references.

## Tactics worth stealing

- Before any shoot, name the one thing. Write it down.
- Hand talent a prop or task: flowers, dominoes, a book, a vintage camera, food.
- Get two steps up. A stool or portable ladder makes high angles easy; high angles flatter most people, low angles can look intentionally rough.
- Use the iPhone 0.5 lens with something close to camera (animal, hand, arm) for exaggerated depth.
- Frame through things: blinds, shutters, a tent opening, mirrors, a glass table from below, a rear-view mirror.
- Cars are the easiest set most people already have.
- Keep the subject still and slow the shutter while others move.
- A cheap articulating arm (around 40 to 50 dollars) clamps anywhere for overhead shots.
- Build a location board for any spot you shoot often.
- Collect lighting refs even if you cannot name the setup; the photographer translates.
- Swap doomscrolling for adding to category boards.
- Weekly homework: pick one reference and recreate it with your own gear, focusing on one variable. Then try a lighting tutorial using only household lights, a ring light or a phone flashlight.
- Make a dedicated IG bookmarks folder for photo, lighting and composition tips.
