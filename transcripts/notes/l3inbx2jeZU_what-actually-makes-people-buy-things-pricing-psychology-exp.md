# What ACTUALLY Makes People Buy Things (Pricing Psychology Explained)

URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=l3inbx2jeZU

Length: about 16.5 min

## The main idea

Pricing is a brand decision, not only a margin decision. He runs through a handful of pricing psychology moves, shows how good brands build a product ladder (entry, core, aspirational, halo) where each tier helps sell the others, and then argues that personas built from real customer data, paired with AI copywriting, let you write separate messages and landing pages for each segment.

## Frameworks and steps

- Anchoring (1:34): the first number someone sees frames every number after it. Hence three-tier pricing, with a high anchor and a low option that make the middle look sensible. The anchor can sit outside your own range, for example a “typical retail” comparison price.
- Price as a quality signal (2:39): buyers can’t judge quality online, so they read price as a proxy. Pricing too low can stop people from trying the product at all. Underpricing hurts the brand, not just margin.
- Charm vs rounded pricing (3:54): prices ending in 9 move more volume in mass and value categories. Premium and luxury do better with round numbers, which tell buyers you aren’t playing games with them.
- Dead zone price points (4:20): shoppers drift toward familiar stops like 49, 59 and 99. A price like 69 or 79 sits in an awkward gap.
- Product mix ladder (4:37):
    - Entry: low risk, easy to try, has to over-deliver so people come back.
    - Core: where most of the revenue and repeat purchases come from.
    - Aspirational: dream pieces that sell in small numbers but lift and anchor everything else.
    - Halo: one-offs, sometimes not really for sale, that set the brand’s ceiling.
- Two common mix mistakes (7:50): launching every tier before the core works, or building only the core with no entry point and no aspirational anchor.
- Data-built personas (8:29): old persona workshops were mostly guesswork, and paid surveys skew toward one type of customer. Build segments from actual Shopify or email data instead, with share of base, lifetime value and repeat rate for each.
- Using personas (12:15):
    - Segment email and SMS by persona and LTV. Push one-time buyers toward a second purchase, and send more to loyal buyers you’re under-mailing.
    - Set up an AI project loaded with persona data, brand materials and a copywriter-style prompt. Use it for ad, landing page, email and SMS copy, product messaging, and deciding which personas can share a message.
    - Ask it for each persona’s pain points to generate ad angles.
    - Build one landing page template and duplicate it per persona with swapped copy and images.
- The ladder applies to services too (15:30): an entry-level audit leads into a monthly retainer.

## Examples and case studies he names

- Software three-tier pricing pages: the standard anchoring setup.
- Luxury candles: a $900 candle on the shelf makes the $200 one feel affordable.
- Quince (heard as “Kint” in the audio): shows typical retail prices next to its own, which he calls a gray-area anchor.
- Best Buy: good, better, best in nearly every category. The best tier rarely sells but props up the middle.
- Streetwear $400 to $500 tees vs his $80 shirts: buyers and boutiques read the high price as designer level even when the cheaper shirt is better made.
- Aesop: a $27 hand soap as the entry point, $60 to $100 skincare as the core, $200 kits and fragrances as aspirational, with the store experience closing the sale.
- Palace: a car in a campaign shoot listed for roughly $40K, as a halo.
- District Vision: a one-off $10K bike at its cycling launch, as a halo.
- Oreo collabs (Swedish Fish, Lady Gaga): the novelty flavors sell modestly but pull people to the shelf and lift sales of the original.
- Apple: SE and AirPods as entry, iPhone and MacBook Air as core, MacBook Pro and Pro Display as aspirational, Vision Pro as halo that makes a $1,600 phone feel normal.
- Loro Piana: $400 to $600 cashmere accessories as entry, $1,500 to $3,000 knits as core, a $12K jacket as anchor.
- Glossier: $18 brow product to get people in, $35 skincare to keep them, $75 fragrance to make it a lifestyle brand.
- Outer Signal (software he uses, not a sponsor): reads a Shopify or email customer base to flag high net worth buyers, influencers and press among recent orders, and builds personas with percentages and LTV. His example brand split into high-performance executive, affluent health investor, wellness professional, athlete and biohacker.
- Granola (sponsor): bot-free AI meeting notes that connect to Claude as live context.
- Wellness professional vs biohacker: same product, opposite motives (work vs self), so each needs its own landing page.

## Tactics worth stealing

- Always show a higher option next to the one you want people to buy.
- Use round prices for premium work and 9-endings only for volume plays.
- Check your prices against dead zones and move to 49, 59 or 99 stops.
- Map your offer onto the four rungs and fill any gaps once the core sells.
- Add a halo piece to a launch for press and ceiling-setting, even if it barely sells.
- Mine your customer list for influencers and UGC creators who already buy from you.
- Write a separate landing page per persona from one duplicated template.
- Split messaging into “buying for work” vs “buying for me” as a first easy segmentation.
- For a studio: offer a low-cost audit or sprint as the entry rung into a retainer.
- His takeaway on cheap pricing: “underpricing is a brand problem not just a margin problem.”
