# The Creative’s Guide to Personal Branding (Full Playbook)

URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qa13iuKa8zA

Length: about 35.5 min

## The main idea

Self-promotion is now a permanent part of working life, and a personal brand is how a creative stands out when hiring is automated, remote and short on mentorship. Most people only need a modest, steady brand that reaches a small, relevant niche. The goal is view quality, not virality, and the payoff comes over years of consistency.

## Frameworks and steps

- Why it matters now (1:17): AI screening, remote work and less mentorship make you a blip on a radar; a brand steers you out. Recognized niche experts get paid a premium over time.
- Flattening of content (3:10): more creators plus sharper algorithms means smaller audiences per topic. The sweet spot for most personal brands is roughly 3K to 10K views a video.
- Four levels, starting at zero (4:28). Level 0: everyone online already has a brand. Level 1: content extends what you do but doesn’t define you (the level he recommends for most). Level 2: you’re known for it online and offline and earn some money from it. Level 3: larger than life, others talk about you (Gary Vee territory).
- Five level-one archetypes (5:39): carousel dumper, documentarian, hobbyist, casual poster, journeyman (a set goal in a set time frame, documented).
- No competition model (9:32): of 100 people who say they’ll start in a niche, about 80 never start, 10 quit early, 5 chase shiny objects, 2 get unlucky. Roughly 3 remain.
- Content toolkit (13:28): video (green screen, talking to camera, quick hits with text over clips, vlogs), carousels (casual dumps or designed), text. Text travels least; reformat a news-plus-opinion post into a designed carousel and it goes further.
- Content market fit (15:22): niche maxing (go deep for a small, valuable audience) weighed against content TAM (how many people care about the topic). Match the TAM to your goal: getting hired, freelance distribution, or even attracting a partner.
- Entertain or give value (17:13): every post must do one or the other for the viewer.
- Pillar system (17:33): four recurring content types; review saves, shares and views monthly; cut, replace or improve the weakest.
- Positioning (18:20): counterposition (opposite production or experience level from the leaders), mirror with a twist (same topics, new delivery or aesthetic), standout demographic (same niche, different audience).
- Feedback loop (20:50): post, analyze, make variants (playbook, personal story, versus), repeat what works.
- View tiers (22:56): 0 to 500, fix the basics (why would anyone care, presentation, stopping power). 500 to 1K, structure, idea, hook, craft. 10K to 50K, study outliers and repeat them. 100K per post, systems and a team.
- Going big (26:44): a marquee media property (podcast, newsletter), signature miniseries, then distribute everywhere.
- Monetization (28:11): contact info in bio, an email list, landing pages with lead magnets. Then services, low-ticket teaching that leads to high-ticket done-for-you work, brand deals, and finally products.
- Time horizon (33:43): 30 days proves you can; 100 days makes it a habit; 1 year is when to expect money; year 2 you know enough to plan; year 3 stay on plan; year 5 it’s a superpower.

## Examples and case studies he names

- Creative directors with 70K followers who mostly post carousel dumps: low effort can still build real traction.
- A cinematographer who shoots car content on the side: a hobby that doubles as a portfolio.
- Young creator Brier: the no-competition idea, plus counterpositioning with lo-fi, basement-shot, newcomer honesty against polished gurus.
- Kane Callaway and the Open Residency creator: proof that very few people make serious marketing content on YouTube.
- Cult Holdings (agency run by his friend Will): standard agency topics framed through cult structures, a mirror with a twist.
- Shelby Sapp: sales training aimed at women in a niche dominated by men, a standout demographic.
- His own Product vs Brand series: capped at about three a month so it doesn’t wear out.
- Maggie Sellers’ Hot Smart Rich podcast: the show became better known than she is.
- His Hyper newsletter: deliberately not named after himself and run with a partner.
- A photography creator who teaches lighting: a $50 guide that leads to a $1,000 Zoom setup session.
- A designer making unsolicited merch for billion-dollar companies: free work that gets brands commenting and hiring.
- Bella (fitness for women over 50), Caroline (“don’t overthink your product”), GradGirl Marketing (beauty marketing curation), Nate Rosen’s Express Checkout (CPG news): niche-niche, idea, and curation formats.

## Tactics worth stealing

- Pick a level on purpose. Level one is enough for most working creatives.
- Turn a strong video into a carousel (and the reverse). Readers and watchers are mostly different people.
- Ride a pop-culture moment fast (his example was the Cracker Barrel rebrand) and tie it to your field.
- Use tribal signals: name the lesser-known brands only insiders know, and your niche will recognize you.
- Apply the office or first-date test to how you show up on camera. Being memorable beats being perfect.
- Cap any signature series so it stays special.
- Repost everything to Shorts, LinkedIn, Substack and YouTube community. It takes about five minutes each.
- Build a 15-minute landing page with a title, subtitle and download (ConvertKit). Point comments or your bio at it.
- “Why they watch is why they buy”: sell what people already come to you for.
- To pitch brands, show the tools you already use in your content so they can picture themselves in it.
- Content formats to test: free work, niche-niche playbook (“X for Y”), would and wouldn’t (as an expert), curation.
