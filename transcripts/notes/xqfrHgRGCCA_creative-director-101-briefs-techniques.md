# Creative Director 101: Briefs & Techniques

URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xqfrHgRGCCA

Length: about 19:15

## The main idea

Creative direction is the work of making things interesting and tying cultural signals into product, marketing and brand decisions, with design as the through line. Oren argues references alone do nothing until you run them through a personal toolkit of techniques that make them original. He pairs that toolkit with a simple, repeatable brief format and a shared-reference workflow between the director, designers and photographers.

## Frameworks and steps

- The creative toolkit (1:15): a set of go-to techniques you apply to any reference or idea so you are adapting, not copying.
- Scale (1:55): make the thing huge or tiny. Oversized objects grab attention; miniature or cute versions work the same way.
- Aesthetic (2:35): a defined visual world for the brand (his example: Western Gothic). Take any reference and re-render it inside your world, with your colors, type and treatments.
- Beauty (3:05): take an ordinary idea and push it to be as pleasing as possible. Different from aesthetic: aesthetic is your style, beauty is polish.
- Transportation (3:15): drop the expected into an unexpected place (runway in the desert, car on a glacier). The mismatch signals something is off and pulls the viewer in.
- Clashing (3:40): smash two references together, e.g. composition from one and look or setting from another, then honestly ask if the result stands out.
- Tension (4:10): apply an uneasy emotion such as shame, fear, anger or ego. He flags the ethics but notes it works; home security selling fear is the obvious case.
- Brief 101 (11:50): title, creative or ad type, purpose and ask, number of assets, destination, ratios, due date, links to content, references, other notes.
- Workflow loop (13:05 to 18:00): brief, pull references, design inside a kit, revise, test, then brief more of what performs.

## Examples and case studies he names

- Jacquemus: uses giant objects in social content, the textbook scale play.
- Dries Van Noten fragrance sampler: a cheap die-cut paper box with a printed color-pattern card behind the vials. It reads luxurious and social-friendly, and works as a down-sell toward the full bottle at around 230 dollars. Beauty applied on a budget.
- July (Australian luggage): known for a trunk form factor, then added a high-gloss finish in bold colors while every competitor is matte and subtle. Beauty layered on an existing aesthetic difference; matte versions shown alongside become the easier choice.
- His Product versus Brand Instagram series: breaks down crowded categories like luggage.
- Valuable sample cargo pants: instead of just showing a sample, they framed it as an archive entry with a numbered code and status, as both a carousel and a video, to build pre-launch hype.
- Shift Global duffel: vintage look plus a functional shoe compartment with its own zipper. Function plus aesthetic, and a natural unboxing moment for owners’ videos.
- Darkroom’s Dark Art service: an embedded designer for a few thousand a month, chosen for taste and training.
- Previous SVP marketing role: an eight-person team ran intake through ClickUp forms so other departments could request work and he could approve it.
- ComplexCon invite: an action-figure-in-packaging graphic, rebuilt with a real model and actual products plus brand stickers (transportation). Then scale: maybe a 10-foot print on the booth or a real blister-pack photo op.
- Call-out ads: he briefed his designer using another creator’s reel on Meta call-out ads, got several style variants and fed them into Advantage+ Shopping campaigns.

## Tactics worth stealing

- Keep a written list of your techniques and run every moodboard reference through it before building.
- Define your brand aesthetic ahead of time so you can reskin any reference fast.
- Use a brief template in Notion or ClickUp with in-progress, completed and archive views.
- Keep a shared Pinterest board with designers so they can pull ideas you have already agreed on.
- Reuse one reference both ways: brief the photographer to capture the angles a designer will need for a planned layout.
- Build kits (for example an email kit with fonts, sizes, buttons, styles) so changes happen in one place.
- When something lands, ask how big it could go or where else it could live. Build a world from recurring elements like stickers.
- For ads, treat any good reel or pin as an instant brief, test, then make more of what wins.
- For products, design one feature that gives owners a reason to film it.
