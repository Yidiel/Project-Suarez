# Freelance vs Agencies vs Creative Studios (becoming an entrepreneur as a creative)

URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yUpTFLvhcGk

Length: about 29.5 min

## The main idea

He lays out the business structures a creative can use to earn beyond a salary: solo freelancing, loose collectives, creative studios, scalable agencies, and “vehicles” (entities that let you release your own work on demand). Each fits a different stage and temperament. Across all of them, the edge is clear communication, documented process, trust between collaborators, and strong social content, which small teams can use to beat big incumbents.

## Frameworks and steps

- His credentials (0:55): years of freelance design, web and strategy work, plus a roughly 40-person product development agency he has since stepped out of and now holds equity in.
- Freelance setup for hard-skill creatives (2:25):
    - Free invoicing (Zoho Books), a one-person LLC for taxes, a solid proposal template with steps and timelines.
    - A weekly status update to every client, every week, read or not: what got done, what’s in progress, blockers, what you need from them.
    - Publish the work. A cheap self-hosted WordPress site with the Salient theme, or Squarespace. Post each project to the portfolio when you send its invoice.
- Soft-skill creatives as conduits (5:49): strategists, PMs and brand people rarely get freelance gigs alone, so they should package a small group of specialists into one offer (for example a fixed-price web package).
- Agency vs collective vs studio definitions (6:39):
    - Agency: services built to scale headcount and revenue, often someone’s full-time job.
    - Collective: people you trust bundling offers for side work or shared projects, with no push to scale.
    - Studio: one or two central drivers who do some services but also make unpaid creative projects and branded products (info, software, physical).
- Why groups beat solos now (7:36): AI and cheaper overseas talent squeeze single-skill roles, while a packaged team saves clients time and costs less than a big agency.
- Open money talk in collectives (11:18): show collaborators the split, for example on a $10K site, $2.5K to the developer, $2.5K to the designer, $5K to the lead for sales, PM and copy. He runs a one-strike rule on reliability.
- Starting and scaling an agency (14:04): he recommends an agency before or alongside a brand because of cash flow. Decide the target size early (a roughly 20-person lifestyle business, a respected 100-person shop, or a major player) and revisit it yearly. Document everything after the third or tenth repetition: onboarding, emails, proposals, weekly updates, hour tracking, deliverable presentation, gifting, hiring, one-on-ones. Founders own new business, and no hired salesperson fixes that.
- Career road map for young creatives (17:07): two to four years at a small brand (about 15 to 40 people), then time at a 20 to 100 person agency, before going entrepreneurial, usually around 26 or 27.
- Vehicles (18:30): an entity with a mission, website, social, newsletter and audience that lets you push out ideas whenever you want. Agencies can be vehicles too if they set aside internal hours for their own projects.
- Spin out and pass the baton (21:01): if one product line hits, spin it into its own company with an operator, or hand a mature brand to a younger creative for equity, the way creative directors succeed each other.
- Two creative temperaments (23:17): perfectionists obsessed with one craft, and idea-hoppers who need many projects. He is the second type.

## Examples and case studies he names

- Valuable Studios (his studio brand): apparel and accessories released to show his range in creative direction and product development, which brought better inbound product and CD work and let him be pickier.
- Handing off his consulting: he passed about $20 to 25K a month of freelance work to another creative for a percentage, a model for shedding work without losing value.
- His engineering agency’s internal hours: spun out a retail brand idea and a smart-battery diagnostics and analytics product.
- Cut 30 film creator: reframed “why I like The Bear’s cinematography” into a six-technique playbook, so the post became bookmarkable and positioned him as the person to hire for that look.
- Cut 30 fitness and marketing creative: owning a niche intersection and jumping fast on the Olympics made her the go-to voice.
- Dark Room (design agency): mixes trend roundups with full spec ad redesigns that show process, so people see them as both informed and capable.
- Big agencies asking him for content help and then not acting on it: proof that small, social-savvy teams can win outsized work.

## Tactics worth stealing

- Send a weekly status email to every client, no exceptions.
- Add work to the portfolio as part of invoicing so it never gets skipped.
- Package soft skills by assembling a small team behind one fixed-price offer.
- Show the money breakdown to collaborators up front.
- Write an SOP once a task has repeated a few times.
- Set aside a slice of agency hours for internal products.
- In content, add “here’s what I’d do” to every breakdown so viewers picture hiring you.
- Turn appreciation posts into playbooks people save and share.
- Pick a niche intersection and react fast to news in it.
- Post spec redesigns of real brands with full process.
- His one-liner on collectives: “creative business is a much more relationship driven business.”
